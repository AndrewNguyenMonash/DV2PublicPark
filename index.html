The Park Next Door

How Australia's eight capital-city councils share out parks, play spaces and places to gather

By Andrew Nguyen · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2

Think of the park at the end of your street: some grass, a playground, perhaps a barbecue. Local councils look after places like this, where many Australians walk, play and meet their neighbours. This page compares the parks of Australia's eight capital-city councils: how much park land each provides for its residents, and what is in those parks. The gap is wide, from about 8 m² of park per resident in the City of Sydney to over 270 m² in Adelaide and Hobart. Then we zoom in on Brisbane, Australia's largest council, to see where its parks are and when they are booked most.

1Why the park next door matters

Where and why active Australians move

Most Australians who exercise do at least some of it in public spaces such as parks & far fewer use a gym or exercise at home. Why people move changes with age: teenagers aged 15-17 are far more likely to be active for fun or to be with friends. Adults aged 65 and over put their health and being outdoors first. Across all ages, more than a third of active people exercise for their mental health, and over two in five for social reasons. A place to move is also a place to feel better and meet people.

Key finding

In 2025, 62 in 100 Australians aged 15 and over were active in public spaces such as parks, about twice as many as at a gym or at home.

Where active Australians exercise

Each square is 1 in 100 Australians aged 15 and over. Filled squares were active in that place at least once in 2025. People can be active in more than one place, so the panels add up to more than 100. Hover over a square for the exact share. Source: AusPlay, Australian Sports Commission, January to December 2025.

Why they move: youngest vs oldest adults

2Eight capitals, eight park networks

How much park land each capital-city council looks after, and for how many people

A council park here is any park, reserve or open space run by a capital-city council; in Canberra, which has no councils, the ACT Government does this job. National parks, state-run gardens and nature reserves are left out. The councils themselves differ hugely in size: Brisbane City Council covers about 1,340 km² and 1.4 million residents, while the City of Perth covers 14 km² and about 36,000. Totals favour big councils, so the column chart divides each council's park land by its residents. By that measure, the small inner-city councils of Adelaide and Hobart lead with about 275 m² each, against just 8 m² in inner Sydney. Park data years range from 2014 to 2026, and Darwin publishes no park outlines.

Key finding

Brisbane City Council looks after about 17,000 ha of parks, more than twice as much as the other six mapped councils combined, and still has 124 m² for each of its 1.4 million residents.

3What's in the park?

Five everyday facilities, compared fairly per 10,000 residents

Playgrounds, BBQs, drinking fountains, toilets and dog off-leash areas are what let people stay longer in a park. Raw counts mostly reflect council size (Brisbane has 3,313 of the 5,866 on the maps), so the heatmap divides each count by residents, or by parks with the Measure menu. Per resident, the small inner-city councils of Adelaide and Perth lead for BBQs and fountains. A grey n/p cell means a council publishes no data for that facility (Brisbane's playgrounds, Sydney's BBQs and four councils' dog areas), which is not the same as having none. Data years run from 2013-16 in Adelaide to 2026; hover over a cell to see each one.

Key finding

Adelaide's parks are the best equipped, with the most BBQs, toilets and drinking fountains on either measure (35 fountains per 10,000 residents), while Canberra's have the fewest BBQs, fountains and dog areas, though the second-most playgrounds per resident.

Only facilities in council parks are counted, except in Canberra and Darwin, where all published ones are. n/p: the council publishes no data, which is not the same as having none. n/a: Darwin has no park outlines to count. 4Up close: Brisbane, Australia's biggest council

Where the council's parks sit, and where facilities cluster

Brisbane City Council is Australia's biggest council by residents (1.4 million). Council parks cover 15% of its suburbs' land, from 99.8% in Mount Coot-tha to none at all in five suburbs. Four of those five are in the far west, a quarter of Brisbane's land where much of the forest is national park or state forest, run by the state. Inner suburbs have little park too: 9.7% within 5 km of the CBD, half the share of the next ring out. The hexagon map counts BBQs, fountains, toilets and dog areas in equal 1 km cells, showing what is within walking distance whatever the suburb lines. Use the Show menu to see one facility at a time: fountains reach 592 hexagons, dog off-leash areas only 157.

Key finding

Inner Brisbane has the smallest share of park land but the most facilities within reach: 93% of hexagons within 5 km of the CBD have at least one, against 23% beyond 15 km.

5When Brisbane books its parks

Two years of permits for fitness classes, parties and events, 2024-2025

Brisbane City Council recorded 63,731 bookings in its council parks over 2024 and 2025, about 87 a day: 91% were permits for paid activities such as fitness classes, and the rest were reservations for private events such as parties. The week has one clear peak on Saturday, while Sunday is the quietest day, averaging 75 bookings. The day starts early too: over half of all bookings begin between 6 and 8am, on weekdays and weekends alike. The seasons matter much less: Every month of 2025 averaged between 85 and 99 bookings a day. Mt Coot-tha Reserve, which fills almost all of the Mount Coot-tha suburb in Section 4, has been the most-booked park every quarter since Q2 2024, while the order below it keeps changing. Click a park in the bump chart to follow it.

Key finding

Saturday is the busiest day in Brisbane's parks: it averages 122 bookings, half as many again as the other days (82), and 70 of the 71 days with 120 or more bookings were Saturdays.

Booked activity by hour of day

Its area is the average number of park bookings that start in that hour, per weekday or per weekend day, so twice the bookings gives twice the area. Hover over a wedge for its numbers. Source: Brisbane City Council, Park - Bookings - Historical (2024-2025).

6What it all adds up to

Three takeaways about the park next door

Parks are where people move

In 2025, 62 in 100 Australians aged 15 and over were active in public spaces such as parks, about twice as many as at a gym or at home. Brisbane's park bookings show the same habit: 91% are permits for paid activities such as fitness classes, and over half start between 6 and 8am.

Your council sets your share

Per resident, the capital-city councils range from 8 m² of park in inner Sydney to about 275 m² in Adelaide and Hobart. What is inside the parks varies just as much, from Adelaide's being the best equipped, to Canberra's which have the fewest BBQs, fountains and dog areas.

Area is not the same as access

Inner Brisbane has the smallest share of park land but the most facilities within reach: 93% of 1 km hexagons within 5 km of the CBD have at least one, against 23% beyond 15 km. Yet its most-booked park is the large Mt Coot-tha Reserve, so big and nearby parks both matter.

Notes on the data

Only council-managed parks are included therefore national parks, state parks and state-run gardens are left out.

Councils publish their data in different years, from 2014 (Adelaide) to 2026. A facility that is not published is not the same as one that does not exist.

Rates per resident count only the people who live in each council. The small inner-city councils (Sydney, Melbourne, Perth, Adelaide and Hobart) also serve many workers and visitors each day, so their parks are shared by more people than these rates suggest.

Park bookings count permits and reservations, not visitors, so Section 5 shows organised use of Brisbane's parks rather than how busy they are.

The City of Darwin's map layers are publicly viewable but state no licence. They are credited to the council here.
